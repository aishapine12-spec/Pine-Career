Alumni Engagement Is Not a Fundraising Line Item. It's Your Institution's Trust Signal.

Why global research says alumni sit alongside parents as the most trusted voice in college choice, and why India is still treating it as a donation drive.
Pine Careers Research Team  |  9 min read

Ask most higher education institutions what "alumni engagement" means, and the honest answer, in practice, is a fundraising calendar. A newsletter a few times a year. A reunion. An ask, dressed up as an update.

This framing is not just narrow. Research on how prospective students and parents actually make college decisions suggests it may be leaving the single most valuable brand asset an institution has sitting almost entirely unused.

Alumni Are Not a Marketing Channel. They're a Trust Source.

A 2025 study of more than 3,400 prospective students and parents, part of Eduventures' ongoing Prospective Parent and Student Research, mapped the sources families actually trust when choosing a university against how much influence each source has on the final decision.

Source: Encoura / Eduventures, "Who To Turn To: The Roles Impacting Students' College Choices," based on 2025 Prospective Parent Research.

The result places alumni and current students in the same "Champions" quadrant as parents and peers, high trust, high influence, and notably ahead of school and admissions counselors, who score high on trust but lower on actual influence over the decision. Teachers and extended family land further out still, in the peripheral quadrant.

In plain terms: a prospective family is more likely to believe what an alumnus tells them about an institution than what the institution's own admissions office says. That is either an enormous asset or a completely wasted one, depending entirely on whether an institution has built any structured way for alumni voices to actually reach prospective families.

Even the "Advanced" Markets Are Mostly Getting This Wrong

It would be a mistake to read this as a India-specific problem. CASE, the body that benchmarks alumni engagement across more than 80 countries, has tracked this consistently: across 2022-2024 surveys, only around 19 to 20 percent of alumni engage in any meaningful activity with their institution in a given year. The other four in five, effectively, go quiet.

What separates institutions that treat this as an asset from those that don't isn't geography. It's deliberateness.

Sources: U.S. News & World Report, 2023-24 alumni giving data across 394 ranked institutions; University Business, reported Princeton giving rate, 2022-23.

The US national average alumni giving rate sits at 8.6 percent. Princeton's sits at roughly 46 to 47 percent, not because its alumni are wealthier or more grateful in some abstract sense, but because institutions like it treat engagement as year-round infrastructure: class agent systems, mid-year touchpoints, giving-day campaigns, not just a reunion-cycle push every five years.

Separately, UK research published via Wonkhe found that graduates who had been involved in three or more campus activities as students had 5.6 times greater odds of later donating than those with no activity involvement at all. The relationship starts before graduation, not after.

Where India's Framework Actually Falls Short

This is where the comparison becomes structural rather than just a matter of effort. CASE's global benchmarking framework tracks alumni engagement across four distinct modes: Philanthropic, Volunteer, Experiential, and Communications, treating giving as one output of a broader relationship, not the relationship itself.

Sources: UGC, "Student Career Progression and Alumni Network" policy document; CASE Insights on Alumni Engagement framework.

India's own regulatory starting point is different. The University Grants Commission's alumni policy document is explicit about its purpose: it is, by its own framing, primarily about "seeking major donations." Universities are encouraged to draw on alumni's professional expertise, leadership, and placement help, but the document's own emphasis, and the name it goes by informally in higher education circles, centres on fundraising.

This produces a familiar pattern: genuine, large-scale success at a handful of elite institutions, IIT Delhi's alumni-funded endowment launched with an initial contribution of Rs 250 crore is a real and significant achievement, alongside a much longer tail of institutions with no structured alumni infrastructure at all. The problem is not that Indian alumni are less willing to engage. It's that most institutions have never given them a mode of engagement beyond writing a cheque.

The Actual Cost of This Gap

A donation-first framework only activates the small fraction of alumni with capacity to give. A four-mode framework activates volunteers, mentors, and communicators too, which is precisely the group whose word-of-mouth sits in the "Champions" trust quadrant that most influences the next prospective family's decision.

Why This Becomes a Brand Problem, Not Just an Advancement One

Rankings measure research output, faculty ratios, citation counts. None of that answers the question a parent is actually asking on behalf of their child: will this institution still be standing behind its graduates ten years from now, and will its network open doors when it matters?

A visible, active, globally distributed alumni network answers that question emotionally, not just statistically. It signals institutional maturity in a way a ranking number cannot: this institution has been producing successful people for long enough, and stays connected to them well enough, that they're still willing to show up for it. For a prospective parent evaluating an unfamiliar or newer institution against an established global competitor, that signal often matters more than a research metric ever could.

Our Take

The data points in one direction: alumni engagement isn't an advancement-office task that happens to have marketing side effects. It is, structurally, one of the highest-trust brand assets a university owns, and most institutions, in India and globally, are leaving most of it inactive.

Frequently Asked Questions

Isn't alumni engagement just another word for fundraising?

That's the most common framing, but it undersells what the research shows alumni actually are: a high-trust, high-influence voice in prospective families' decision-making, not just a donor base. CASE's own global benchmarking framework tracks four distinct modes of engagement, Philanthropic, Volunteer, Experiential, and Communications, treating giving as one possible outcome of a relationship, not the relationship's entire purpose.

Do alumni actually influence prospective students' and parents' decisions?

Yes, measurably. A 2025 study of over 3,400 prospective students and parents found that alumni and current students sit in the same high-trust, high-influence "Champions" quadrant as parents and peers, ahead of school and admissions counselors on actual influence over the final decision. A prospective family is, in practice, more likely to believe an alumnus than the institution's own marketing.

What is the CASE four-mode framework?

CASE (the Council for Advancement and Support of Education) benchmarks alumni engagement across more than 80 countries using four modes: Philanthropic (giving), Volunteer (time and service), Experiential (mentoring, guest teaching, event participation), and Communications (staying reachable and responsive). Institutions that only track the Philanthropic mode are measuring a fraction of their actual alumni relationship.

How is India's approach to alumni engagement different from global standards?

India's regulatory starting point is narrower. The UGC's own alumni policy document is explicit that its primary purpose is "seeking major donations," a single-mode, philanthropic-first framing, compared to CASE's four-mode global standard. This produces genuine, large-scale success at a handful of elite institutions, but a much longer tail of institutions with no structured alumni infrastructure covering volunteering, mentorship, or communications at all.

Can a university improve alumni engagement without a large advancement team?

Yes, but only if the starting point is mapping, not headcount. Most of the engagement gap isn't caused by insufficient staff; it's caused by institutions not knowing where their alumni are, what they do, or how to reach them in the first place. Building that foundational visibility is a data and infrastructure problem before it's a staffing problem, and it's possible to solve at meaningful scale without a large team, provided the mapping work happens first.

Does alumni engagement actually affect university rankings or accreditation?

Indirectly, but genuinely. Alumni engagement itself is rarely a heavily weighted ranking criterion on its own, but graduate outcomes and destination data, information that active alumni engagement makes far easier to collect accurately, do feed into employability rankings and accreditation reviews in most markets. An institution with strong alumni visibility can produce better evidence for those processes than one relying on outdated or incomplete graduate records.
