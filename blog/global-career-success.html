Global Career Success Isn't About Where You Study. It's About How You're Prepared.

Rankings tell you about research output and reputation. They don't tell you whether a graduate can actually get a job. Here's what the research says does.
Pine Careers Research Team  |  8 min read

Every year, millions of students choose a university partly, sometimes mostly, on the strength of a ranking. QS. Times Higher Education. National league tables. These rankings measure real things: research output, faculty reputation, citations, academic reputation surveys.

What they don't measure directly is the question every one of those students is actually asking underneath the decision: will this get me a good career?

The Ranking Myth

This distinction is not a rhetorical flourish. The Times Higher Education Global Employability University Ranking, compiled separately from THE's main academic rankings, surveys employers across more than 40 countries specifically on which universities produce the most "work-ready" graduates. It exists as a separate ranking precisely because employer perception of graduate readiness and academic reputation are not the same measurement, and don't always move together.

A university can rank highly on research output and still underperform on the outcome that matters most to the student sitting in the lecture hall today: what happens after graduation.

What the Research Actually Shows Moves the Needle

If rankings alone don't explain graduate outcomes, what does? A growing body of academic research, including systematic reviews spanning dozens of peer-reviewed studies, points to a consistent pattern, and it isn't the one most marketing materials lead with.

Synthesized from systematic reviews of graduate employability interventions (ScienceDirect, 2025; Discover Education, 2026) and longitudinal Career Registration Methodology research (MDPI, 2024, n=1,653 students). Qualitative synthesis of findings, not a single quantitative meta-analysis.

Practical experience outperforms everything else

A 2025 systematic review of graduate employability interventions found that programmes offering genuine practical experience, internships, placements, live industry projects, had the strongest positive impact on employability of any intervention type studied. Employability-skills workshops without a practical component showed mixed results by comparison, and government-mandated programmes with weak institutional buy-in were largely inconclusive.

Career readiness compounds when it's tracked

A 2024 study using Career Registration Methodology followed 1,653 students longitudinally, tracking their career planning and participation in employability activities throughout their entire enrolment, not just in a final-year careers module. The finding: engagement in employability and professional experiences was the most significant factor associated with full-time employment, job offers, and graduates' own sense that their qualification held real value.

Mentorship works, but it's not a silver bullet

A 2026 systematic review specifically on mentorship and graduate employability found consistent evidence that mentorship builds soft skills, professional identity, and confidence. What it found far less evidence for was mentorship translating directly into employment outcomes on its own. The honest conclusion: mentorship is a developmental mechanism, not a guarantee, and works best as one part of a structured system rather than a standalone intervention.

Why This Matters

None of these findings say a good degree doesn't matter. They say that a degree plus practical experience, tracked career readiness, and structured mentorship produces a measurably different outcome than a degree alone, regardless of how the issuing institution ranks academically.

Why the Same Degree Produces Wildly Different Outcomes

The gap between "prepared" and "unprepared" doesn't just show up between institutions. It shows up between entire countries, for graduates with functionally the same qualification.

Sources: HESA Graduate Outcomes Survey (UK, class of 2022/23), reported by UKCISA; QILT Graduate Outcomes Survey (Australia, undergraduate, 2024).

In the UK, the gap between domestic and international graduate employment rates is small, around 2 percentage points, according to the most recent HESA Graduate Outcomes data. In Australia, the equivalent gap for undergraduates was over 21 percentage points in 2024, according to QILT's official Graduate Outcomes Survey.

Same broad idea, an international student earning a degree abroad, producing outcomes that differ by a factor of ten depending on the market. That is not explained by student quality. It's explained by structural factors: visa work rights, the depth of employer relationships built into the education system, and how much career infrastructure a graduate has access to before they need it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a university's ranking predict how employable its graduates are?

Not directly. Academic rankings like QS and THE's main league tables measure research output, faculty reputation, and citations. That's why a separate ranking exists specifically for employability: the Times Higher Education Global Employability University Ranking surveys employers across more than 40 countries on which universities produce the most work-ready graduates. The two rankings don't always move together, so a strong academic rank is not a substitute for evidence of graduate outcomes.

What actually improves graduate employability, according to the research?

The strongest, most consistently supported factor is direct practical experience: internships, placements, and live industry projects. A 2025 systematic review of employability interventions found these had the greatest positive impact of any intervention type studied. Structured, ongoing career readiness tracked throughout a student's enrolment was the second strongest factor, per a 2024 longitudinal study of 1,653 students. Employability workshops without a practical component showed only mixed results by comparison.

Is mentorship enough on its own to improve career outcomes?

Not reliably, on its own. A 2026 systematic review of mentorship and graduate employability found strong evidence that mentorship builds soft skills, professional identity, and confidence, but far weaker and more context-dependent evidence that it translates directly into employment outcomes by itself. The research describes mentorship as a developmental mechanism, not a guaranteed employment outcome, meaning it works best as one part of a structured system rather than a standalone fix.

Why do international graduates have such different employment outcomes in different countries?

The gap varies enormously by country, and it isn't explained by student quality. In the UK, the difference between domestic and international graduate employment rates is around 2 percentage points (HESA, class of 2022/23). In Australia, the equivalent undergraduate gap was over 21 percentage points in 2024 (QILT). That difference reflects structural factors, visa work rights, the depth of employer relationships built into each country's education system, and how much career infrastructure a graduate can access before they need it, not the graduates themselves.

What does "career readiness" actually mean in practice?

In the research cited here, career readiness refers to a graduate's demonstrated exposure to employability and professional experiences, tracked progressively across their entire enrolment rather than addressed only in a final-year careers module. This includes practical work experience, structured career planning activity, and engagement with employer-facing opportunities, measured over time, not as a single checkpoint before graduation.
